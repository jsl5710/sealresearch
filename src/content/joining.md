## Why this page exists

Applying to a PhD program often feels like a guessing game: you are asked to write a statement for a person whose actual priorities you cannot see. This page exists to remove some of that guesswork. What follows is what I am genuinely looking for, what I can genuinely offer, and what I expect — written plainly enough that you can decide whether SEAL is a good fit before you spend a weekend on an application.

If something here is unclear or seems to contradict what you have heard elsewhere, tell me. That is useful information about the page, not about you.

## What SEAL works on

SEAL studies how **language variation shapes both the harms and the defenses of modern AI systems**. Most safety work is built and evaluated on Standard American English. We ask what happens to everyone else — speakers of dialects, of low-resource languages, of creoles, of the roughly seven thousand languages that AI infrastructure was not designed around.

Our work sits in four areas:

- **Multilingual NLP and low-resource AI** — language technology for languages with little data and less investment
- **Trustworthy AI, safety, and information integrity** — detection, robustness, and the failure modes of safety guardrails
- **Fair, ethical, and equitable AI** — who is disadvantaged by a system, measured rather than asserted
- **Human-centered AI** — systems evaluated with the people they affect, not only on benchmarks

We work **across modalities — text, audio, and multimodal systems**. That is not a list of buzzwords; it follows from the question. A safety guardrail can fail on written dialect, and it can fail again on accented speech for reasons that have nothing to do with the text: prosody, disfluency, and speech recognition that was never built for the speaker. Our recent work on audio language models — over-refusal in AOR-Bench, spoken hallucination detection in Lost in Speech — came directly from following that failure across the modality boundary.

We care about **both the human and the technical sides of technology**, and we do not treat them as separate problems. A guardrail that fails for Caribbean English is a modelling problem and a question about who was in the room when it was built. Work here is expected to hold both at once.

The signature thread is **dialect-conditioned vulnerability**: safety systems that hold for one variety of a language and fail for another, and the evaluation infrastructure needed to see that failure at all. If you have read DIA-HARM or BLUFF, you have seen the shape of it.

## Who does well here

I am looking for **hard-working, dedicated students who are not afraid to take on a new problem and find the solution**. Much of what we do has not been done before — there is no tutorial for building an evaluation set for a language nobody has served. That means being comfortable at the point where the literature runs out and the work starts.

But working hard is not by itself what I am asking for. I want people who **work hard and support each other**.

SEAL is, deliberately, something like a small United Nations: people from different countries, languages, disciplines and backgrounds, working on problems that none of us could see the whole of alone. That only works if we hold two things together — **we strive for excellence, and we look after each other while doing it.** A lab that produces good papers and burns through people is not a success, and I will not run one.

Concretely, that means:

- **Curiosity past your own subfield.** The interesting problems here sit between NLP, security, and the social sciences, and nobody arrives fluent in all three.
- **Raising problems early.** A result that does not replicate, an experiment that will not finish, a plan that has stopped making sense — all of these get easier the sooner they are said out loud.
- **Healthy working relationships.** Disagreement about ideas is welcome and expected; treating people badly is not. Rest is part of the job, not a reward for finishing it.
- **Making the lab better for the people after you.** Norms, documentation, mentoring newer students. This is real work and I treat it as such.

I am also explicit about what I am *not* screening for: a polished publication record before you arrive, a named institution on your CV, or a statement that sounds like it was written by someone imitating a professor. Which brings me to —

## Please do not write what you think I want to hear

Statements written to flatter an advisor are easy to recognise and they work against you. I would much rather read an honest account of a problem you actually find interesting — even one outside my areas — and your own reasoning about why it matters, than a paragraph that mirrors my abstracts back at me.

Tell me what you have worked on, what went wrong with it, and what you would do differently. That tells me more about your research trajectory than a list of things you admire.

## How I advise

Advising is not one relationship. Over a PhD I will be, at different moments, a collaborator, a critic, a coach, and an advocate for you in rooms you are not in. Which of those is useful changes as you become more independent, and part of my job is to notice when it has changed.

What makes this work is **explicit expectations and early communication** — on both sides. I try to say what I think rather than hint at it, and I will ask you to do the same. The [lab handbook](#handbook) sets out the norms I am committing to: authorship, meeting cadence, feedback, time off, and what to do when something goes wrong. It is a living document and you are welcome to read it before you apply.

I will push you, and I will also step back when stepping back is what helps you grow. Getting that balance wrong in either direction is a failure of mine, not yours, and it is worth telling me when it happens.

## How to apply

### PhD students

Apply to the **PhD in Information Science** at CU Boulder. Admission is made by the department, not by me individually — I cannot admit you outside that process.

1. Submit your application to the Information Science PhD program.
2. **Name me** as a prospective advisor in your statement of purpose, and say specifically why SEAL.
3. Email me a CV before you submit, so I can flag your application.

### Master's and undergraduate researchers

Rolling, and the surest route into this kind of research if you are already at CU Boulder. Email me with (1) your resume, (2) a short paragraph on what draws you to this work, and (3) how many hours a week you have.

### Postdoctoral researchers

No active call at present. A brief expression of interest is welcome and I will keep it on file.

## On emailing me

You are welcome to email with questions. I read everything, though during application season replies are slow — if you do not hear back, it is volume, not judgement.

**Not emailing me will not hurt your application.** I want to be clear about that, because the applicants most likely to assume otherwise are usually the ones with the least access to people who have done this before.

Please do not cold-email my students to ask them to vouch for you. Ask them about the lab if you meet them at a conference; that is different.
